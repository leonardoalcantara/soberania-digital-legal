 Key points

 The Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026 (the Bill) will extensively amend the Online Safety Act 2001 Act to introduce a digital duty of care (DDoC) for persons responsible for online services as well as strengthening the capacity of the eSafety Commissioner to regulate online services, content and conduct. 

 Under the DDoC, persons responsible for online services ‘must take appropriate action to ensure a safe online environment, including by taking effective measures to mitigate risks identified in risk assessments…’. 

 The DDoC and other related requirements will replace the existing co-regulatory framework of industry codes and standards (and the Basic Online Safety Expectations reporting obligations) following a 12-month transitional period. 

 Under the Bill, the Minister will be able to require that online services provide Australian users with ‘user empowerment tools’. An exposure draft legislative instrument outlining minimum standards for user empowerment tools and choice of preferred feed has been released for consultation . 

 The Bill’s provisions differ from the exposure draft released in September 2026, including by removing the ministerial power to add categories of ‘seriously harmful material and conduct’ and by inserting a new category of ‘excluded material’. 

 Other changes from the exposure draft include the insertion of an age assurance and access control measure requirement and more restrictive provisions in relation to the use of ‘sock puppet identities’ in the proposed data access scheme. 

 The Bill has been referred to the Senate Environment and Communications Legislation Committee for inquiry and report by 20 November 2026.

 Purpose of the Bill

 The purpose of the Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026 (the Bill) is to amend the Online Safety Act 2001 (the Act) to: 

 strengthen the capacity of the eSafety Commissioner (Commissioner) to regulate online services, material and conduct 

 impose a Digital Duty of Care (DDoC) and related requirements on persons responsible for online services. 

 The Bill will also amend the Act and other associated legislation to repeal existing industry codes and standards following a 12-month transitional period.

 In her second reading speech (pp. 6–7), the Minister for Communications, Anika Wells stated: 

 The core of the digital duty of care is to provide a safe online environment, where all Australians are protected from seriously harmful and illegal content. It moves the safety net upstream to the people who design the product to stop the harm, before it ever reaches an Australian user… 

 The digital duty of care also sets minimum standards for the features and tools that tech companies use. Online service providers will have to conduct annual risk assessments of the harms the features on their service could cause to Australians—features like algorithmic recommender systems, the ability to livestream incognito from your glasses, or the ability to artificially generate text and pictures. Then they will need to put systems in place to prevent those risky features from causing harm to Australians, because the architecture matters… 

 And, through My Feed, My Way, social media companies will have to provide user empowerment tools that give people over the age of 16 choice about what they see in their feed.

 Structure of the Bill

 The amendments of the Bill are contained in 3 schedules: 

 Schedule 1—Notices for certain material and disclosure of information 

 Schedule 2—Digital duty of care 

 Schedule 3—Repeal of online content scheme industry codes and standards 

 Background

 A statutory review of the Act conducted by Delia Rickard was provided to Government in October 2024. The review made 67 recommendations including many relating to legislative reform. In particular, the review recommended (pp. 54–56): 

 Recommendation 4 

 That Australia adopt a singular and overarching duty of care that encompasses due diligence, and is underpinned by safety by design principles, risk assessment and mitigation. 

  Recommendation 5 : 

 The harms that should be highlighted for attention under a duty of care should at a minimum include: 

 Harms to young people including child sexual exploitation and abuse (including grooming), bullying and problematic internet use

 Harms to mental and physical wellbeing including threats to harm or kill, or attacks based on a person or group of people’s protected characteristics, such as sex, gender, sexual orientation, race, ethnicity, disability, age or religion

 Instruction or promotion of harmful practices such as self-harm/suicide, disordered eating and dares that could lead to grievous harm

 Threats to national security and social cohesion, such as through promotion of terrorism and abhorrent violent extremist content; and

 Other illegal content, conduct and activity.

 The review stated ‘an essential part of meeting the duty of care for online service providers would be to undertake regular risk assessments of their services’ (p. 57). It recommended that entities with the greatest reach or risk ‘should also be required to provide an annual report detailing their risk assessments, risk mitigations and how successful they have been to the regulator’ (Recommendation 6). 

 In November 2024, the Albanese Government announced it would be legislating a DDoC to ‘place the onus on digital platforms to proactively keep Australians safe and better prevent online harms’. It stated that the proposed DDoC would ‘complement the Government’s decision to legislate a minimum age of 16 for access to social media’. 

 In April 2026, the Government’s response to the statutory review indicated an intention ‘to implement, wholly or partially, or further consider 64 of the 67 recommendations’ (p. 2). It stated that ‘[a] duty of care will support broad, risk-based and proportionate regulation of all digital service providers and provide a legislative framework that can better accommodate changes in technology and services …’ (p. 3). 

 On 20 July 2026, a joint ministerial media release outlined ‘a series of AI safety priorities to help ensure AI works better for Australians …’ These included legislating a DDoC ‘that puts the onus on AI companies to build in safety by design and proactively address potential harm …’

 On 8 September 2026, the Government released an exposure draft of the Bill. Consultation feedback was sought by 22 September 2026.

 On 12 September 2026, the Government announced a ‘My Feed, My Way’ initiative under which social media users would be able to opt-in or opt-out of ‘having their default feed include personalised content recommended by the algorithm’. 

 The UK Online Safety Act

 One of the international precedents underpinning the statutory review ’s recommendation for a singular DDoC was the Online Safety Act 2023 (OSA) passed by the United Kingdom in October 2023 (p. 53). The OSA contains a range of duties of care for social media companies and search services to implement including duties to protect users from illegal content and content harmful to children. A House of Lords Committee is inquiring into the OSA as ‘serious concerns have been raised that the regime is currently ineffective’. 

 Policy position of non-government parties/independents

 Coalition

 When the exposure draft was released the Coalition announced it would oppose the Bill ‘in its current form’ due to concerns about political censorship. 

 Following the Bill’s introduction, the Shadow Minister for Communications and Digital Safety, Senator Sarah Henderson described it as ‘a legislative dog’s breakfast which the Coalition cannot support’. She reiterated concerns that the Bill would facilitate government censorship, highlighted ‘dangerously vague definitions of online harm’ and argued the Bill took an inconsistent approach to regulating pornographic material. Senator Henderson stated that the Bill contained ‘a confusing web of overlapping obligations’ as well as noting the compliance burden on small businesses and the potential misuse of the Bill’s proposed powers for the Commissioner and researchers to use ‘sock puppet accounts’. 

 Australian Greens

 The Australian Greens welcomed the Government’s initial announcement of the DDoC. In October 2026, the Australian Greens indicated they ‘were willing to work with’ the Government but they would seek to strengthen the measures in the Bill: 

 The Greens want the legislation to include a right to private action, allowing individuals to take tech giants like Meta to court and receive compensation when they are harmed online. They are also calling for stronger guardrails on addictive algorithms and fines tied to a percentage of Big Tech’s global revenue. 

 One Nation

 One Nation representatives have been critical of the Bill. In September 2026, a One Nation media release described it as ‘censorship, pure and simple’ and ‘the sort of thing you'd expect a dictatorship to be flirting with, not a mature democracy’.

 Independents

 A number of independent parliamentarians have expressed support for greater user control over social media algorithms including creating a right for users to opt-in and opt-out. 

 In November 2024, former independent MP Zoe Daniel introduced a private member’s Bill (which has since lapsed) imposing an overarching duty of care onto large online providers. At its introduction, independent MP, Monique Ryan spoke in support of the Bill . 

 Key stakeholder views

 Public debate regarding online safety regulation often prompts considerable interest. The statutory review of the Act received a large number of submissions including from a range of interest groups, corporates, charities, industry groups, academics and think tanks. For example, Reset.Tech Australia advocated for a duty of care approach to move ‘the focus beyond the content layer of the digital world to focus on the underlying systems’ (p. 4). 

 While the departmental consultation on the exposure draft has not made submissions publicly available, some submitters released their contributions. For example, the Australian Human Rights Commission was supportive of the exposure draft but raised areas ‘where further consideration would strengthen the framework and better support its objectives’ including that the categories of harmful material and conduct ‘do not include hate speech’. 

 A joint submission from a range of organisations and academics , including the Alannah & Madeline Foundation, strongly supported the exposure draft’s central provisions and recommended ‘targeted amendments to further strengthen’ the proposed legislation including making the best interests of children the primary consideration for online services when assessing and mitigating risks. 

 While supporting the objective of reducing online harm, the Business Council of Australia submission had significant concerns about how the exposure draft of the Bill ‘would operate as drafted’ focusing on implementation and compliance burden issues (p. 1).

 The Institute of Public Affairs submission on the exposure draft highlighted its earlier concerns that a DDoC would ‘place an extraordinary burden on freedom of speech online by imposing a vague and subjective standard of harm and by incentivising over-compliance among internet services faced with heavy penalties and an over-zealous regulator’ (p. 1). 

 On 22 September 2026, the United States embassy indicated that it held ‘serious concerns about the Australian government’s stated goal of mandating broad duty of care requirements that dictate how platforms manage content and users’ feeds’. 

 The Senate Committee inquiry into the Bill is seeking submissions by 30 October 2026. 

 Key issues and provisions

 Schedule 1—Notices for certain material and disclosure of information

 Part 1—Take down notices 

 Division 1—Fake nude material 

 Item 5 of Part 1 inserts proposed section 86B which provides for the Commissioner to give the provider of an app distribution service or internet search engine a written removal notice requiring the provider to remove the app, remove links to the app or website or remove advertising for, or information about, or access to the app or website. 

 This removal notice could be made where the Commissioner is satisfied on reasonable grounds, that an app or website ‘was designed for the predominant purpose of generating fake nude material’ and ‘is being predominantly used by end-users to generate fake nude material’. The term ‘fake nude material’ is not defined in the Act or the Bill. 

 Under proposed section 86C , the provider must comply with a removal notice ‘to the extent the provider is capable of doing so’ within 24 hours after the notice is given or a longer period allowed by the Commissioner ( item 5 ). The civil penalty for non-compliance would be 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000). The Commissioner’s decision to issue a removal notice may be reviewed by the Administrative Review Tribunal ( item 11, proposed subsection 220(10B) ) 

 Division 2—Removal notice powers

 Under the Act’s cyber-bullying and cyber-abuse complaints framework, complainants who wish the Commissioner to issue a removal notice to providers must have evidence that a complaint regarding the relevant material has previously been made to the provider.

 The amendments made by Division 2 will allow the Commissioner to waive this requirement if satisfied that the service ‘does not have a functional or accessible complaints mechanism’ or ‘requiring the complaint to be made would create a reasonably foreseeable risk of further harm’. Further, the Commissioner will also be able to issue a removal notice without a complaint to the provider being made if a removal notice had been made in relation to the material in the last 6 months and the material has been reposted even if it has been altered. 

 The period of time which providers have to comply with a removal notice will also be shortened from 48 hours to 24 hours. 

 Division 3—Links to certain material

 Under the online content scheme provisions of the Act, the Commissioner may give a link deletion notice to internet search engine service providers where end-users in Australia can access class 1 material (generally material which has been, or is likely to be, classified as refused classification )( section 124 ). 

 The amendments in Division 3 will expand the capacity of the Commissioner to give link deletion notices to internet search engine service providers in relation to cyber-bullying material, non-consensually shared intimate images and cyber-abuse material after a removal notice has been given in relation to the image or material. 

 The civil penalty for failure to comply with a link deletion notice requirement in relation to cyber-bullying material, non-consensually shared intimate images and cyber-abuse material will be 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000). 

 Part 2—Disclosure of information

 Currently, the Commissioner may disclose information to a list of specific federal authorities (such as the Australian Federal Police) when satisfied that ‘the information will enable or assist the authority to perform or exercise any of the authority’s functions or powers’ ( paragraphs 212(1)(a)-(f) ). Item 51 will expand the capacity to disclose information in these circumstances to any ‘ Commonwealth entity (within the meaning of the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013 )’. 

 Part 3—Updating references to the Regulatory Powers (Standard Provisions) Act 2014

 Part 3 amendments insert an abbreviated definition for the Regulatory Powers (Standard Provisions) Act 2014 (Regulatory Powers Act) and make related consequential amendments. 

 Part 4—Increased penalties

 Part 4 increases a range of civil penalties in the Act for persons who do not comply with removal notices, blocking notices, remedial notices, link deletion notices, app removal notices and remedial directions from 500 penalty units ($182,000) to 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000). The EM repeatedly states the reason for increasing these civil penalties is ‘to reflect the potential significant harm that might result…’ to affected parties from non‑compliance (pp. 40–45).

 Higher penalties may apply to corporations (who are likely to be the main regulated entities). Subsection 82(5) of the Regulatory Powers Act provides that for a body corporate pecuniary penalties may be up to ‘5 times the pecuniary penalty specified for the civil penalty provision’. This increase accords with the statutory review which recommended that ‘civil penalties for non-compliance with removal notices should be increased to a maximum of $10 million for companies’ (Recommendation 53). 

 The amended civil penalty provisions cover a spectrum of regulated activity under the Act—some of which may be applied to end-users. For example, section 91 concerns failure to comply with removal notice relating to cyber-abuse material targeting an Australian adult. Potentially, 6,000 penalty units may be perceived as a disproportionate maximum civil penalty to achieve deterrence when applied to a natural person. For context, under the Crimes Act 1914 , where a person is convicted of an offence with a penalty of life imprisonment a court has the discretion to instead ‘impose, instead of, or in addition to, a penalty of imprisonment, a pecuniary penalty not exceeding 2,000 penalty units’ ( subsection 4B(2A) ). 

 Items 84 and 85 will also double the civil penalties for non‑compliance with directions to comply with industry codes and non-compliance with industry standards (subsections 143(2) and 146(1) ) from 30,000 penalty units ($10,920,000) to 60,000 penalty units ($21,840,000).

 Part 5—Effect of industry codes and standards

 The proposed DDoC and other requirements will not commence for a 12-month period and during this time the existing industry codes and standards regime will continue in force. 

 Items 90 and 91 amend sections 140 and 145 which deal with the registration of industry codes and the determination of industry standards respectively. These sections include certain timing requirements for consultation (industry codes) and periods before the Commissioner may be satisfied that an industry code is deficient (in order to determine an industry standard). The amendments insert new subsections which provide that, for industry codes and standards which were registered before commencement, the validity of these codes and standards is not affected and is taken to never have been affected ‘only because a period allowed for the purposes of this Subdivision was shorter than required’. 

 The EM does not clarify the rationale for these amendments (p. 46). However, if the registration of industry code or determination of an industry standard had failed to meet the timing requirements it appears the amendments would retrospectively validate their registration or determination respectively.

 Schedule 2—Digital duty of care

 Part 1—Amendment of the Online Safety Act 2021

 Item 1 will repeal and replace section 3 expanding the objects of the Act. The new objects will be to improve and promote online safety for Australian by: 

 imposing a duty on persons responsible for online services to ensure a safe online environment; and

 protecting, and acting in the best interests of, Australian children online; and 

 providing complaints schemes or mechanisms to quickly investigate, remove and remediate illegal or seriously harmful material; and 

 providing for an eSafety Commissioner to promote, monitor, investigate and enforce compliance with this Act.

 Similarly, item 2 will repeal and replace section 4 to expand the simplified outline of the Act to incorporate the amendments of the Bill. In particular, the new simplified outline section includes: 

 A person responsible for an online service has a digital duty of care to ensure a safe online environment for persons in Australia, including by protecting adults and children from seriously harmful material and conduct and protecting children from material, conduct and design features that are harmful to them.

 A person responsible for an online service also has other obligations that are separate but related to their digital duty of care. These obligations include conducting risk assessments to assess and mitigate reasonably foreseeable risks for the service and, where required, providing user empowerment tools that give users greater control over how design features operate and influence their online experience.

 Simplified outline and application

 Currently Part 2 of the Act sets out the functions and powers of the Commissioner. Item 8 repeals and replaces this with proposed Part 2—Digital duty of care . 

 Proposed section 25 contains a simplified outline listing the key obligations of the DDoC. Proposed section 25A concerns the application of proposed Part 2 providing that it does not apply to State and Territory agencies and authorities to the extent they provide or control online services and that the Commissioner’s remedial directions cannot require persons ‘to take an action to the extent that it has no impact on users in Australia of the online service concerned’. 

 Key concepts

 The scope of the DDoC is determined by a number of key concepts. 

 Online service

 Proposed section 25B outlines a broad meaning for the term online service which includes internet carriage services supplied to the public, social media services, hosting services, internet search engine services, app distribution services, services which generate material using artificial intelligence (AI) using an internet carriage service, and services that manufacture, supply, maintain or install equipment which enables communications in Australia using an internet carriage service. 

 The person responsible for an online service is the person who provides the service or ‘the person who exercises the greatest degree of day-to-day control over the service’ (except for services that manufacture, supply, maintain or install equipment etc). 

 The Minister may, by legislative instrument, exempt, and conditionally exempt, online services or a class of online services from all or parts of proposed Part 2 if satisfied that the services ‘pose little risk to users of the service or services in Australia’. 

 Safe online environment

 Proposed section 25C defines a safe online environment as an online environment in which: 

 persons in Australia are protected from seriously harmful material and conduct

 children in Australia are protected from:

 material and conduct that is harmful to children and 

 harms associated with access or exposure to design features of online services

 if the service concerned is a social media service—children in Australia who have not reached 16 years of age do not have access to, and are not exposed to, design features of the service that have negative behavioural impacts.

 The framing of this key definition reflects the social media minimum age obligation which requires providers of age-restricted social media platforms (platforms) to take reasonable steps to prevent Australian children under the age of 16 from having accounts. 

 A social media service is currently broadly defined in section 13 as a service with the key characteristics that its sole or primary purpose is to enable online social interaction between end-users and is accessible in Australia. Under the Bill, the Minister may determine, by legislative instrument, which online services are taken or not taken to be social media services ( proposed subsection 25C(2) ). 

 Seriously harmful material and conduct

 Proposed section 25D provides the definition of seriously harmful material and conduct . This definition contains an exhaustive list of harmful material which the EM describes as ‘closely aligned with material and conduct that would likely be considered Class 1A material (content that is or would be refused classification in Australia) … or is illegal under the Commonwealth Criminal Code Act 1995 ’ (p. 54). 

 Relevantly, item 6 also adds a clarification of the term harm to section 5:

 harm : a person can be harmed by online material or conduct even if the person does not experience the material or engage in the conduct as a user of an online service 

 One category which appears to expand the definition of ‘seriously harmful material and conduct’ beyond existing regulatory prohibitions is ‘material or conduct that encourages, promotes, incites or instructs gender-based violence’ ( proposed paragraph 25D(1)(h) ). Item 6 inserts a definition of gender-based violence in section 5 of the Act as meaning ‘any form of physical or non-physical violence, harassment, abuse or threats, based on gender, that results in, or is likely to result in, harm, coercion, control, fear or deprivation of liberty or autonomy’. There is no definition of ‘gender’ in the Bill. Previous legislative use of the term ‘gender-based violence’ also left the term ‘gender’ undefined. The Acts Interpretation Act 1901 provides in section 23 that ‘words importing a gender include every other gender’. 

 Harmful to children

 Proposed section 25E defines the term harmful to children . The following material and conduct fall within this definition: 

 pornography

 material or conduct that encourages, promotes or instructs disordered eating 

 material or conduct that encourages or promotes hostile attitudes towards women or gender equality 

 material or conduct that glorifies crime or encourages dangerous stunts or seriously harmful practices

 abuse, harassment or bullying 

 any other material or conduct, or combination of material and conduct, that may cause serious harm to children.

 The scope of some of these categories is unclear. Key concepts such as where material ‘encourages’, ‘promotes’, ‘instructs’ or ‘glorifies’ are not defined.

 The EM notes that the term serious harm (which is relevant to the definitions in both proposed sections 25D and 25E ) relies on an existing definition in section 5 of the Act meaning ‘serious physical harm or serious harm to a person’s mental health, whether temporary or permanent’ (p. 59). Additionally, the definition of serious harm to a person’s mental health includes serious psychological harm and serious distress but ‘does not include mere ordinary emotional reactions such as those of only distress, grief, fear or anger’ ( p. 59 ).

 Excluded material

 The definitions of both ‘seriously harmful material and conduct’ and ‘harmful to children’ do not include excluded material . This term is defined in proposed section 25F covering a range of categories. 

 Some of these listed categories of excluded material may be uncontroversial such as material related to ‘conducting scientific, medical, academic or historical research’ and material relating to ‘protecting or promoting public health and safety’. Other categories of excluded material may relate to contested areas or subject-matter. For example, while ‘professional news material’ is a category of excluded material, potentially the associated definitions of professional news material and news material may exclude some forms of journalism. The EM notes that ‘professional news material’ must be in listed formats, produced by persons subject to media codes or similar rules and the person must have editorial independence from the subjects of the person’s news coverage ( EM , pp 61–62).

 In contrast, concerns may also be raised that other categories of excluded material may be too open to interpretation such as material relating to ‘reducing harms to individuals’. 

 Another category of excluded material is material ‘produced, performed or disseminated for a literary, artistic or educational purpose’. However, this category requires the material also be ‘not contrary to the public interest’. The term ‘public interest’ is not defined in the Bill or the Act. There is also a catch-all category of excluded material for ‘material that is in the public interest’. The Bill also does not contain factors or matters to be considered in assessing the ‘public interest’.

 Material ‘produced for the sole or dominant purpose of entertaining individuals’ contained in television programs, radio programs, audiovisual content and classified films or computer games will also be a broad excluded material category. However, material ‘generated by a person using a feature of a computer game’ will not be excluded material. The EM states that this is ‘intended to capture user-generated material, where enabled by the feature of a computer game (including chat functions), whose nature or impact may not align with the computer game’s classification’ (p. 60). 

 While there are categories of excluded material regarding material in connection to the duties and functions of ‘public officials’ and material related to ‘court or tribunal proceedings’ there are not express categories of excluded material for parliamentary proceedings, political communications or cultural and religious communications. 

 Design features that have negative behavioural impacts

 A component of the key definition of safe online environment is that children under 16 years of age do not have access or exposure to service ‘design features that have negative behavioural impacts’. This is clarified in proposed section 25G which provides that the following design features of an online service have negative behavioural impacts : 

 a recommender feature

 a logged-in feature

 a design feature determined by the Minister by legislative instrument under proposed subsection 25G(2) . 

 The provision also includes definitions of a recommender feature and a logged-in feature and related definitions for endless-feed feature , feedback feature and time-limited feature . 

 Digital Duty of Care

 Proposed section 26 contains the DDoC obligation. It provides that ‘a person responsible for an online service must take appropriate action to ensure a safe online environment, including by taking effective measures to mitigate risks identified in risk assessments conducted in accordance with [proposed] section 27’. 

 This differs from the exposure draft legislation which used a standard of ‘reasonably practicable’ rather than ‘appropriate action’ and incorporated mandatory requirements (p. 31). 

 Proposed section 26A lists a range of matters to be taken into account in determining if actions or measures are ‘appropriate’ or’ effective’ for the purposes of complying with the DDoC. These include the ‘extent to which the action or measures achieve or would achieve a safe online environment’, specific matters in relation to ‘seriously harmful material’ and ‘material that is harmful to children’, the impact on the security of the online service and ‘whether the action or measures are or would be proportionate to the level of risk’. However, it includes a clarification that ‘the cost of the action or measures must not be taken into account’. 

 A significant exception to the DDoC is in relation to ‘lawful private communications’. The DDoC ‘does not require any action to be taken in relation to lawful communications occurring in private solely between consenting adults’ ( proposed section 26B ). This exception may be relevant to whether online services are obligated to monitor or take actions in relation to messages or material sent directly between end-users in order to comply with the DDoC (particularly encrypted messaging services). The EM states (p. 68): 

 For the sake of clarity, large group chats or servers with potentially hundreds or thousands of participants are not considered private for the purposes of this section. This exemption also does not apply to private communications that are not lawful. Relevant harm mitigation mechanisms, such as child sexual abuse material detection tools or video and call disruption tools, should be applied where there is a risk of serious illegal harm. 

 The maximum civil penalty for a person who fails to comply with the DDoC will be 60,000 penalty units ($21,840,000) ( proposed section 26C ). The Commissioner may issue formal warnings if the Commissioner reasonably believes that a person has failed, or is failing, to comply with the person’s DDoC. The Commissioner may also give persons remedial directions requiring person ‘to take specified action directed towards ensuring that the person does not fail, or is unlikely to fail, to comply with that duty in the future’. Failure to comply with a remedial direction is subject to a civil penalty of 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000) ( proposed subsection 26E(3) ). 

 As noted above, higher penalties may apply to corporations. Subsection 82(5) of the Regulatory Powers Act provides that for a body corporate pecuniary penalties may be up to ‘5 times the pecuniary penalty specified for the civil penalty provision’. The EM states (p. 68): 

 Maximum penalties should be strong in proportion to the size of a service to be effective. Setting the penalty amount at 60,000 units is important to signal a strong deterrent for non-compliance and ensure that regulated entities comply with the Duty. 

 Other requirements

 Risk assessments

 As noted above a component of the DDoC is ‘… taking effective measures to mitigate risks identified in risk assessments conducted in accordance with [proposed] section 27’. Proposed section 27 provides that for the purposes of complying with the DDoC ‘a person responsible for an online service must ensure that written risk assessments are conducted for the service’. These risk assessments must: 

 identify ‘reasonably foreseeable risks’, the material or conduct that gives rise to those risks, the design features of the service that give rise to those risks, the other systems or processes that give rise to those risks and persons who may be affected by those risks

 assess the likelihood and potential severity of those risks

 document the measures the person has implemented, or proposes to implement, to mitigate those risks

 record an ‘assessment of the expected effectiveness’ of the measures (and other matters)

 provide for regular review and reassessment of the effectiveness of those measures

 meet any additional requirements determined by the Commissioner (which the Commissioner may determine by legislative instrument).

 The risk assessments must be conducted ‘at least’ annually or at shorter intervals if determined by the Commission (by legislative instrument) and ‘before making any significant changes to the service that could introduce new or additional risks’. 

 User empowerment tools 

 Proposed section 27A provides that the Minister, by legislative instrument, may require a specified online service to provide users of the service in Australia with a specified user empowerment tool and prescribe requirements for, and in relation to, the tool.

 A user empowerment tool is defined ‘as a tool that allows a user of an online service to manage the way design features of the service operate for the user, including, for example, by providing control over the kind of content that is recommended for the user’ ( proposed subsection 27A(5) ). 

 This appears intended to be a power with broad ministerial discretion as proposed subsection 27A(2) provides the ‘online service or user empowerment tool may (without limitation) be specified by reference to any matter’. However, ‘the Minister must be satisfied that the tool is necessary to protect users of the service in Australia from harms associated with access or exposure to a design feature of the service’ and ‘the Minister must not take into account any material that has been, is or will be accessible to, or delivered to, a user using the service’ ( proposed subsections 27A(3) and (4) ). This latter restriction may have been included to mitigate the risk this provision is challenged on the basis that it infringes the implied freedom of political communication.

 As noted above, the Government announced a ‘My Feed, My Way’ initiative in September 2026. With the introduction of the Bill, the Government also released an exposure draft of the Online Safety (User Empowerment Tools) Determination 2026 which may be made under proposed section 27A . It includes a requirement that social media or age‑restricted social media platforms using a recommender feature for content ‘must provide a user empowerment tool that allows users to determine their preferred feed’ (p. 2). 

 Age assurance and access control measures

 Proposed section 27D provides that: 

 where there is a reasonably foreseeable risk that a child could access, or be exposed to, pornography or material that describes or depicts specific fetish practices or fantasies

 each person responsible for an online service must ‘where technically feasible and reasonably practicable’ ensure that users of the online service in Australia have: 

 a choice of 1 of at least 2 appropriate age assurance measures

 access control measure. 

 Item 3 inserts related definitions into section 5 for: 

 access control measure means a design feature of an online service that prevents an Australian user who is not age assured as an adult from accessing, attempting to use, or being exposed to:

 pornography; or

 material that describes or depicts specific fetish practices or fantasies. 

 age assurance measure means a method, or a range of methods, used by an online service for determining or assessing a user’s age.

 This obligation was not contained in the exposure draft of the legislation. The EM states (p. 76): 

 The purpose of this provision is to ensure that platforms trigger age assurance systems where the provider identifies a reasonably foreseeable risk that children may access specified material on their service. This aligns with requirements under the existing industry codes set to be repealed by the Bill, while remaining consistent with the broader risk-based architecture of the [DDoC]. 

 The scope of this obligation is unclear as several terms remain undefined such as ‘pornography’ and ‘depicts specific fetish practices or fantasies’. It is not clear why the proposed provision did not draw on established classification categories such as class 1 material and class 2 material .

 Complaint and dispute process

 Proposed section 27G allows the Commissioner to determine a class of prescribed online services by legislative instrument (in consultation with the Privacy Commissioner). Persons responsible for prescribed online services must: 

 have complaint and dispute processes that are available equally to all individuals in Australia 

 comply with any requirements determined by the Commissioner in relation to those processes.

 Compliance and remedial direction provisions for the other requirements

 The compliance provisions for the other requirements of the DDoC share common elements. Persons contravening the risk assessment, user empowerment tool, age assurance and access control measures obligations may be liable for maximum civil penalties of 60,000 penalty units ($21,840,000). 

 The Commissioner may also issue formal warnings where the Commissioner reasonably believes a person has failed, or is failing, to comply with these obligations and remedial directions requiring the person to take specified action directed towards ensuring that the person does not fail, or is unlikely to fail, to do those things in the future. Failure to comply with a remedial direction may be subject to civil penalties of 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000). 

 The exception is the complaint and dispute process obligation which has a civil penalty of 6,000 penalty units for both the contravention and the failure comply with a remedial direction ( proposed subsection 27G(5) and 27H(3) ).

 Supporting provisions

 The Commissioner will also be empowered to undertake several other functions which support the DDoC and other requirements. These include: 

 publishing guidelines to assist persons responsible for online services to meet their DDoC ( proposed section 28 )

 requiring persons responsible for online services to:

 provide information in relation to the functions of a feature, system or process of online service, or 

 grant nominated persons access to services to report to the Commissioner about the features, systems and processes of the service (with compliance subject to formal warnings and civil penalty of 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000)) ( proposed section 28A )

 further empowering the Commissioner to give advice to the Minister on a range of matters related to proposed Part 2 which contains the DDoC and the other requirements ( proposed section 28C ). 

 The Minister will also be required to seek the Commissioner’s advice before making legislative instruments related to exemptions for online services, social media services covered, design features that have negative behavioural impacts and user empowerment tools ( proposed section 28B ) 

 Functions and powers of the eSafety Commissioner

 Item 27 inserts proposed Division 1A—Establishment and functions which establishes the Commissioner and sets out the Commissioner’s functions in Part 11 . Proposed section 166B repeats many of the existing functions of the Commissioner (currently set out in section 27 ) with amendments to reflect the imposition of the DDoC and other requirements. For example, the function to ‘formulate, in writing, guidelines for the taking of reasonable steps to prevent age-restricted users having accounts with age-restricted social media platforms’ has been expanded to also include formulating ‘guidelines for the taking of reasonable steps’ to ‘prevent children who have not reached 16 years of age from having access or exposure to design features of online services that have negative behavioural impacts’ ( proposed paragraph 166B(1)(r) ). 

 However, the amendment to proposed paragraph 166B(1)(n) indicates a shift in the functions of the Commissioner. The Commissioner’s function under this paragraph will be changed from ‘monitor compliance with this Act’ to ‘monitor, investigate and enforce compliance with this Act’. This emphasis on enforcement activity is reflected in proposed section 166C which requires the Commissioner to prepare an annual statement of priorities in relation to monitoring and enforcing compliance with the Act. This will not be a legislative instrument. 

 Item 28 changes the title of Division 4 of Part 11 to ‘Division 4—Commissioner’s powers and other matters’. A number of the other amendments in the retitled Division 4 incorporate the Bill’s changes into existing provisions concerning annual reporting requirements, delegations and the use of contractors. 

 Item 29 inserts proposed section 177A which provides that the Commissioner has the power to do all things necessary or convenient to be done for or in connection with the performance of the Commissioner’s functions (replicating existing section 28 ).

 Item 36 inserts proposed section 183AA which will allow the Commissioner to ‘publish on the Commissioner’s website a formal warning issued under this Act’. This appears to be a new capability for the Commissioner. 

 Transparency reports and repeal of the Basic Online Safety Expectations

 A key component of existing online regulatory framework is the Basic Online Safety Expectations (BOSE) contained in Part 4 of the Act. This allows the Minister, by legislative instrument, to determine BOSE for social media services, relevant electronic services and designated internet services (service providers). Core expectations to be included in the ministerial determinations (including that services ‘will take reasonable steps to ensure that end‑users are able to use the service in a safe manner’) are contained in section 46 . 

 However, while the Commissioner can require service providers to report periodically and non-periodically on their compliance with the BOSE, the BOSE do ‘not impose a duty that is enforceable by proceedings in a court’ ( subsection 45(4) ). 

 Item 13 will repeal Part 4 . This means BOSE reporting obligations for service providers will continue during the 12-month transitional period until replaced by proposed transparency reports (discussed below) and the risk assessment requirements of the DDoC. 

 Item 38 inserts proposed Part 12A —Transparency under which the Commissioner may require the persons responsible for online services to prepare and give transparency reports about the safety of the service for Australians and to publish information about online safety related operations or activities. 

 Civil penalties will apply to persons who do not comply (‘to the extent that the person is capable of doing so’) with the Commissioner’s transparency report notices or publication legislative instruments. The maximum civil penalty will be 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000) and the Commissioner may issue formal warnings ( proposed sections 192H, 192J, 192M, 192N ). 

 Compliance and investigations

 Part 13 of the Act contains the Commissioner’s information-gathering powers. Item 42 will insert proposed Division 2—Compliance and investigations . The proposed Division will give the Commissioner powers to require persons to give information or evidence, produce documents and answer questions under oath or affirmation. The proposed Division will replace Part 14 which currently contains the Commissioner’s investigative powers (repealed by item 43 ).

 These powers will apply where the Commissioner has reason to believe that a person is capable of giving information or evidence, producing documents or answering questions relevant to: 

 the investigation of complaints relating to cyber-bullying material , intimate images , cyber‑abuse material or the online content scheme 

 any compliance with, or a possible contravention of, the Act (other than a provision of Part 4A which deals with the social media minimum age framework). 

 Non-compliance with the requirements to give information, produce documents, answer questions or take an oath or make an affirmation will be an offence with a maximum penalty of 12 months imprisonment. Non-compliance will also be subject to a civil penalty of 1,000 penalty units ($364,000) ( proposed section 204 ). 

 The non-compliance penalties will not apply if the person is a journalist and complying ‘would tend to disclose the identity of a person who supplied information in confidence to the journalist’ ( proposed subsection 204(5) ). 

 Persons will not be excused from giving information or evidence, producing documents or answering questions on the basis of self-incrimination. However, any information, evidence or answer given or document produced will not be admissible in civil or criminal proceedings ( proposed section 205 ). 

 The EM notes the intention of amendments is ‘to support the effective administration’ of the DDoC and the information gathering powers are based on similar provisions in the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (p. 93). 

 Research and data access

 Item 43 would repeal and replace Part 14 . Under the research and data access regime in proposed Part 14 , a person responsible for an online service may be required to give approved researchers access to data for the purposes of undertaking online safety related research.

 Data access schemes 

 An approved researcher would be a person employed by an Australian university and is approved under legislative rules and meets any other prescribed requirements ( proposed subsection 205B(1) ). 

 Online safety related research is research undertaken by an approved researcher to the extent it is relevant to online safety of Australian users of online services, approved by a relevant human research ethics committee, is of a kind prescribed by the legislative rules to be in the public interest and meets any other prescribed requirements ( proposed subsection 205B(4) ). 

 Proposed section 205C provides for legislative rules (the data access rules ) to be made requiring persons responsible for online services ‘to give approved researchers access to data for the purposes of, or in connection with, undertaking online safety related research’. In making the data access rules, the Minister must be satisfied that ‘the access to data provided by the scheme would be reasonable in all of the circumstances’ ( proposed subsection 205C(3) ). 

 The data access rules broadly frame the kinds of data to which approved researchers must be given access including ‘the decision-making algorithms of a feature, system or process of an online service used to select, order, recommend, promote, restrict or moderate material, accounts or search results’ and ‘the circumstances in which such features, systems or processes require human review or intervention’ ( proposed subsection 205C(4) ). There are some restrictions: 

 the data access scheme must not require a person responsible for an online service to give approved researchers access to data other than data relevant to the online safety of users of the service in Australia ( proposed subsection 205C(5) ) and

 the data accessed by approved researchers must not be used by the researcher for the purposes of obtaining a commercial benefit or advantage ( proposed subsection 205C(6) ). 

 However, the framework of civil penalties, formal warnings and remedial directions for the data access rules only appears to apply to persons responsible for online services and not approved researchers. Civil penalties of 6,000 penalty units ($2,184,000) may apply for contravening the data access rules or the Commissioner’s remedial directions ( proposed sections 205D and 205F ). 

 Sock puppet identities

 Proposed Division 3 establishes a framework for the use of sock puppet identities by approved researchers and the Commissioner. A sock puppet identity is a false or fictitious identity assumed by a person in the course of using, or carrying out any activity in relation to, an online service ( proposed section 205G ). 

 Proposed sections 205H and 205J allow sock puppet identities to be assumed by approved researchers ‘for the purposes of undertaking online safety research’ and by the Commissioner ‘for the purposes of performing the Commissioner’s functions or exercising the Commissioner’s powers under [the] Act’. This may be undertaken ‘despite anything to the contrary in any agreement, contract or deed’ (presumably as some online service expressly restrict the use of sock puppet identities).

 Approved researchers and the Commissioner must use sock puppet identities only in accordance with proposed section 205K . This provision contains a list of authorised activities that may be undertaken using sock puppet identities: 

 creating and using an account to access an online service

 observing, downloading or making a record of any material accessed using an online service 

 engaging with an online service or a person responsible for the service 

 engaging with a user of an online service (but only to the extent necessary to prevent closure of an account with the service) 

 testing the features of an online service 

 testing an online service’s response to actions taken using the online service.

 Additionally, the Commissioner may use sock puppet identities ‘to test whether an online service is able to, or allows users to, generate (including by means of artificial intelligence) material the production, dissemination or possession of which is an offence against a law of the Commonwealth, a State or a Territory’.

 However, while proposed section 205K appears to be a restrictive provision which limits the scope of the use of sock puppet identities, the EM describes it as including a ‘non-exhaustive list’ (p. 98). This may be a legacy of the exposure draft which included the phrase ‘(without limitation)’ in a previous version of the provision (p. 63). 

 Proposed section 205L provides that a person will not be liable to a civil action, suit or proceeding for acts done, or omitted to be done, in good faith under the sock puppet provisions ( sections 205H and 205J ). The EM states that ‘protection from civil proceedings in the use of sock puppet identities is intended to enable the Commissioner and approved researchers to conduct prescribed online safety related research without fear of civil proceedings for example through the breach of an online platform’s terms of service’ (p. 99). 

 Potentially, immunity from civil proceedings may be insufficient legal protection for approved researchers and the Commissioner depending on the use of the sock puppet identities. In particular, some of the activities outlined in proposed section 205K (such as testing features or downloading material) could involve dealing with illegal content. 

 Part 2—Amendments of other Acts

 Subsection 39(1A) of the Age Discrimination Act 2004 (ADA) provides exemptions from the unlawful discrimination provisions for persons who are acting in direct compliance with laws listed in Schedule 2 of the ADA. This includes specified provisions of the Act listed in item 8A.

 Item 52 repeals and replaces item 8A in Schedule 2 to add proposed Part 2 (containing the DDoC) and proposed paragraphs 166B(r) and (s) (which are the Commissioner’s functions to formulate and promote guidelines preventing user-restricted users from having accounts with age‑restricted social media platforms or preventing children under 16 years of age from having access or exposure to ‘design features of online services that have negative behavioural impacts’). The amendment repeals the existing reference to the Commissioner’s guidelines making functions (which will be repealed) but retains the reference to Part 4A .

 Item 53 amends the definition of ‘enforcement body’ in subsection 6(1) of the Privacy Act 1988 to include the Commissioner. Enforcement bodies have a range of exemptions from privacy obligations when conducting enforcement related activities.

 Schedule 3—Repeal of online content scheme industry codes and standards

 Part 1—Amendments of the Online Safety Act 

 Currently, Part 9, Division 7 of the Act facilitates online regulation through industry-developed codes and Commissioner-determined industry standards. The eSafety Commissioner’s website maintains a register of these online safety codes and standards . 

 This industry co-regulatory regime will be replaced by the DDoC framework following a 12‑month transitional period. The amendments of Part 1 will repeal the references to industry codes and standards in the Act including the whole of Part 9, Division 7 ( item 5 ).

 Part 2—Amendments of other Acts

 Items 15 and 18 make consequential amendments reflecting the repeal of industry codes and standards to section 130L of the Broadcasting Services Act 1992 and section 36 of the Interactive Gambling Act 2001 , respectively.

 Items 16 and 17 make amendments to subsection 273.9(5) and subsection 474.24(4) of the Commonwealth Criminal Code which provide ‘conduct in good faith’ defences to the offences in Division 273—Offences involving child abuse material outside Australia and Division 474—Telecommunications offences (in respect of child abuse material). 

 The amendments repeal the defences which refer to the industry codes and standards and replace them with defences where a person engages in conduct ‘in good faith’ for the sole purpose of ‘manufacturing or developing, or updating, content filtering technology (including software) for the purposes of compliance with the person’s, or another person’s, digital duty of care …’
